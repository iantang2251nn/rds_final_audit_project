\section{Input and Output}

\subsection{Civil Comments and identity-subgroup coverage}

The system audited here is the third-place solution to the 2019 Jigsaw \emph{Unintended Bias in Toxicity Classification} Kaggle competition \citep{borkan2019nuanced}, trained on the public release of the Civil Comments corpus. Civil Comments operated as a third-party comment-hosting platform for English-language news sites between 2015 and 2017; the public dataset contains approximately 1.8M comments collected when the platform shut down. The audit runs on the private test set ($n = 97{,}320$).

Each comment was annotated by crowd workers along two parallel tracks. The toxicity track records a primary \texttt{toxicity} label and five subtype labels (\texttt{severe\_toxicity}, \texttt{obscene}, \texttt{identity\_attack}, \texttt{insult}, \texttt{threat}, \texttt{sexual\_explicit}), each as the fraction of raters who applied the label. The identity track records twenty-four attributes spanning gender (4 columns), sexual orientation (4), religion (7), race/ethnicity (5), and disability (4), each as the fraction of identity-annotators who marked the identity as referenced. Following Civil Comments convention, an identity is treated as ``mentioned'' when its column value is at least~$0.5$.

At threshold~$0.5$, the nine identities evaluated by the competition's bias-aware metric---\texttt{male}, \texttt{female}, \texttt{homosexual\_gay\_or\_lesbian}, \texttt{christian}, \texttt{jewish}, \texttt{muslim}, \texttt{black}, \texttt{white}, and \texttt{psychiatric\_or\_mental\_illness}---together account for $11{,}003$ mentions. The fifteen identity columns the metric does not evaluate---including \texttt{transgender}, \texttt{bisexual}, \texttt{asian}, \texttt{latino}, \texttt{hindu}, \texttt{buddhist}, \texttt{atheist}, and every disability category other than \texttt{psychiatric\_or\_mental\_illness}---account for $807$ mentions in aggregate. All evaluated subgroups have $n \geq 238$ at this threshold; all excluded subgroups have $n \leq 217$. The selection is therefore sample-size driven, with a cutoff falling between those two values---a fact we return to in \S5.3.2 when interrogating the metric's identity-inclusion choice. Per-group base rates of the toxicity label among evaluated identities span $[0.0996, 0.3233]$ (\texttt{christian} to \texttt{black}), a range whose magnitude becomes binding for the impossibility-result discussion in \S5.2.

\subsection{Output: toxicity score and thresholding}

The system produces seven floating-point outputs per comment, each in $[0, 1]$: a primary toxicity score $\hat{Y}$ and six subtype scores. Each score is the model's estimate of the probability that a randomly drawn Civil Comments rater would apply the corresponding label to the comment. The competition metric and our \S5.1 accuracy analysis treat $\hat{Y}$ as a continuous score and use AUC-style measures. Our \S5.2 fairness analysis thresholds $\hat{Y}$ at $s_{HR} = 0.5$ to recover a binary moderation decision and report the resulting confusion-matrix metrics. We adopt $0.5$ as a deliberately conventional choice---the default a deployed moderation pipeline would inherit from any binary classifier---so the threshold-side analysis is reproducible and a reader who prefers a different operating point can substitute it without re-running the model. Per the algebra of \S5.2, the impossibility result holds at any non-trivial threshold given the base-rate spread reported above.

\subsection{The toxicity label as a measurement choice}

The toxicity label we audit against is itself the product of a measurement choice: it is the fraction of crowd-worker raters who applied the ``toxic'' label to a given comment. This formulation embeds three commitments that downstream fairness analysis inherits. First, it treats toxicity as an aggregable property of the text rather than a property of the rater--text dyad: a label value of $0.4$ does not distinguish ``a coherent minority of raters found this toxic'' from ``most raters disagreed about whether it was toxic at all.'' Second, training and evaluation both binarize the score around $0.5$, so subgroups whose lived sense of what counts as a slur or a microaggression is a minority view among the rater pool are systematically under-counted as harmed parties. Third, the rater pool is not demographically balanced to the populations the labels are \emph{about}; the literature on annotator identity in subjective NLP tasks has documented substantial label drift as rater demographics change. The audit that follows respects the label as given, but every fairness gap we report should be read modulo this upstream measurement choice---a different rater pool or a different aggregation rule could move some of the gaps materially.
